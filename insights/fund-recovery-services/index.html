Fund Recovery Services: What Helps — and What Is a Second Scam

After a loss, strangers often offer “fund recovery services” that guarantee results for an upfront fee. Some are a second scam. This guide separates lawful next steps from recovery-scam red flags.

What legitimate help looks like

Real counsel or investigators review evidence, explain bank, card, crypto, regulator, and civil options, and are clear that recovery is never guaranteed. They do not ask you to send cryptocurrency to “unlock” a phantom balance.

Mike Levy Law offers confidential consultations for material losses — evidence first, then pathway advice. We do not sell a recovery deposit product.

Recovery-scam red flags

  • Guaranteed return of funds or “we already found your money.”
  • Upfront crypto / gift-card / wire fees to release a recovery.
  • Pressure to act in minutes or stay off the phone with your bank.
  • Impersonation of lawyers, police, or exchanges.

If someone contacts you unsolicited after a broker loss, assume risk until proven otherwise.

  1. Preserve wallets, chats, and payment proofs.

How the second scam finds you

Victim lists are sold and resold. Within days of a loss, “recovery specialists” may call knowing your name, the platform, and even the amount — details that feel like proof of legitimacy. They are proof of a leaked database, nothing more. Some operations are run by the same group that took the first loss, wearing a second costume.

The scripts are consistent: your money was “located” on a blockchain or in a seized account, a refundable “release fee” stands between you and payout, and a lawyer, police officer, or regulator is available to “confirm” — all played by the same room.

A five-question test for any recovery offer

  1. Did they contact you first? Unsolicited recovery contact is the single strongest predictor of fraud.
  2. Do they guarantee a result, or name a percentage? No legitimate professional does.
  3. Is payment demanded before work begins — especially in crypto, gift cards, or wire?
  4. Can you verify the person independently: bar number checked with the bar association, company in the official register, office you can call back on a published number?
  5. Do they discourage you from talking to your bank or the police? That is isolation, and it is deliberate.

One failed answer is a warning. Two is a decision.

What a real review does in the first conversation

Legitimate fund recovery work starts with documents, not a wallet address. A first look should identify how you paid, how recent the transfers are, whether a card or wire clock is still open, and whether any named company can actually be served. It should also say when the file is weak: no payee, no hashes, a platform that vanished, or an amount too small for tracing. That candour is the product. The consultation is free, and it exists to validate prospects and feasibility from the level of proof — not to sell a recovery.

Anyone who opens with “we already located your funds” before seeing a statement is describing a script. The same is true of a demand to pay in crypto, gift cards, or a wire to a personal account before work begins. Public agencies and real counsel do not unlock a balance that way. If you already paid a second party, keep those receipts too: the recovery pitch can be its own loss, with its own dispute window.

How to separate tracing from a promise

Blockchain tracing can show where coins moved and whether they touched an exchange that collects identity documents. It cannot force that exchange to pay you. An exchange freeze, if it happens, usually follows a law-enforcement or court request, not a private email. Civil claims need a defendant with a name and, ideally, assets. Reporting to the FBI IC3, Action Fraud, or a national cyber unit creates a record those requests can cite.

Keep a single evidence index: date, amount, rail, destination, and the file name of the proof. Investigators and banks both work faster from an index than from a zip of unnamed screenshots. Do not install remote-access software for a stranger who says they will “recover the wallet.” That is a common second step after the first loss.

Questions worth answering before you hire anyone

Ask who the contracting party is, which jurisdiction they work in, and what they will not do. Ask whether they ever request a payment to release funds. Ask for the specific rail they think is still open in your dates. A vague plan to “contact the exchange” without hashes is not a plan. Compare that answer with your own documents before you share more personal data than the file needs.

A worked file: dates, amounts, and what still mattered

Files that match “fund recovery services” rarely arrive as a single payment. A typical one we can actually assess looks like this. A first transfer of about $8,750 left by a credit card. A second, about $11,200, followed 9 days later by a credit card, after a chat said the account was “almost verified.” The dashboard then showed a larger figure than either transfer. That larger figure is not the claim. The claim is the two amounts that left an account the client controls, tied to the domain in the browser bar.

On day 2 a withdrawal was requested. The reply did not refuse in one sentence. It added a condition that was not on the page at signup, then a second condition when the first was questioned. The useful record is the pair of messages with timestamps, plus the statement lines, plus the URL. People lose days rewriting this from memory. Export it once, name the files by date, and keep the originals. Screenshots of a phone gallery are weaker than an exported chat that still shows the other party’s handle.

What decided feasibility was not the on-screen profit. It was which clock was still open. The card leg could be disputed only if the issuer’s window had not closed. The wire leg depended on whether a recall had been requested while the money could still be sitting at the receiving bank. Any crypto leg depended on whether a hash existed at all. A free consultation can only validate prospects at the level of that proof. If the proof is the dashboard and nothing else, the honest answer is that the file is thin, even when the story is true.

Searchers reach this page with different wording — fund recovery services, recovery scam, crypto recovery service, recover stolen funds, upfront recovery fee, fake fund recovery — but the intake is the same shape. Bring the chronology on one page, the statements, the domain, and the refusal. Do not bring a promise that the balance will be paid, and do not pay a new invoice to make the balance “withdrawable.” If a second company has already contacted you to recover the first loss, keep that approach separate: it has its own dates and its own risk, and it should not be mixed into the original dispute narrative.

Use the exact phrase you would type into a bank form, not the marketing name. If the statement says one company and the website says another, lead with the statement. Clone sites and payment agents are how a good story gets attached to the wrong defendant. Regulators and warning lists are worth checking against the domain, and worth attaching if the domain is named, because they corroborate notice. They do not replace the payment dispute, and they do not create a refund by themselves.

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers on next steps, deadlines, and when a confidential consultation may help.

Are fund recovery services legitimate?

Some investigative and legal services are legitimate. Many cold-call “recovery” firms are not. Guarantees plus upfront unlock fees are strong warning signs of a recovery scam.

Should I pay a fee to get my money released?

No. Do not pay a stranger to unlock stolen or withheld funds. That pattern is how victims lose a second time.

What should I do instead?

Preserve evidence, contact your payment providers, file reports, and — if the loss is material — request a confidential consultation with counsel who will not demand a recovery deposit.
